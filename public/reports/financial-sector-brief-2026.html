Section OneContext

The Inclusion Paradox

Ghana leads Africa in mobile money infrastructure — and still leaves 65% of MSMEs without formal credit.

Ghana has built remarkable financial infrastructure. With 74.1 million registered mobile money accounts, 896,000 agents, and $192 billion in transaction volume in 2024, Ghana holds the number-one ranking on the GSMA Mobile Money Index. The Bank of Ghana is among the most progressive regulators in the developing world.

Yet the infrastructure has not translated into economic security. Only 35% of MSMEs access formal credit. Seventy percent fail within five years — not from lack of hustle, but from lack of the working capital they need. Commercial lending rates exceed 27%, and for the millions excluded from banks, informal moneylenders charge 100% or more. Meanwhile, $6.65 billion in annual remittances flows 90% into consumption, bypassing the productive investment that would build household assets and business capacity.

"The challenge is not infrastructure — it is translating access into economic security for the market trader, the susu saver, and the diaspora family sending money home."

The sector's dysfunction creates a clear mandate. BRIDGE's position — with deep relationships in market infrastructure, diaspora networks, and community-level financial systems — is uniquely suited to bridge the gap between Ghana's world-class rails and the economic outcomes they have yet to deliver.

Financial Inclusion Depth: Access vs. Utilisation
Ghana's access headline (97%) masks a sharp gradient: MSME formal credit access is 35%, financial literacy 32%, and insurance penetration just 12% of GDP. The opportunity is not more infrastructure — it is activation of what exists.
02
Section TwoCredit System

The $2.2 Billion Credit Gap

MSMEs contribute 70% of Ghana's GDP. Thirty-five percent of them have access to the formal credit they need to grow.

Ghana's MSMEs — 92% of all registered businesses, 70% of GDP, 80% of employment — face systematic exclusion from the formal credit system. The barriers are structural, not cultural. Land tenure insecurity means 98% of land titles cannot serve as collateral. Bank documentation requirements exclude the 80% of enterprises operating informally. And commercial lending rates above 27% make formal credit unaffordable even when accessible.

The result is a $2.2 billion annual financing gap: $4.9 billion in demonstrated MSME demand against $2.7 billion in supply from banks and MFIs combined. Excluded from formal systems, traders turn to informal moneylenders charging 100%+ annual interest. A trader borrowing GH₵5,000 for 90 days pays back GH₵6,250 — a cost that consumes 15–30% of business profits.

MSME Financing: Supply vs. Demand Gap
Formal and MFI credit supply totals $2.7B against $4.9B in demonstrated MSME demand — a $2.2B annual gap that forces 65% of excluded businesses into informal credit at 100%+ annual rates.

Section ThreeDigital Finance

Mobile Money: The Infrastructure Ghana Built

$192 billion transacted in 2024. $934 billion projected by 2033. The rails exist — the productive use cases do not yet.

Ghana's mobile money system is a genuine global success story. MTN Mobile Money leads with 60%+ market share, complemented by AirtelTigo, Vodafone, and Zeepay. The 896,000-agent network reaches rural and peri-urban communities that banks never will. The Bank of Ghana's regulatory framework earned Ghana the number-one GSMA ranking globally.

The frontier is not transaction volume — it is financial deepening. Mobile money in Ghana remains primarily a payments and transfers rail. The products that would convert access into wealth-building — working capital credit, microinsurance, investment products, diaspora channeling — are underdeveloped. BRIDGE's Market Financial Services Platform is designed to sit on top of these existing rails and deliver the product layer that activates them for economic security.

Mobile Money Transaction Volume Growth & Trajectory
Ghana's mobile money volume has grown from $68B (2020) to $192B (2024) and is projected to reach $934B by 2033. The infrastructure is built — the financial product layer on top of it is the remaining gap.
04
Section FourCommunity Finance

The Susu System: 850 Collectors, Millions of Savers

Ghana's informal savings infrastructure reaches communities no bank can serve. It is the foundation for alternative credit scoring — not the competition.

Ghana's susu system — daily savings collection rooted in community trust — serves millions of Ghanaians who do not appear in any formal financial dataset. Over 850 registered collectors manage hundreds of clients each, predominantly women (70–90% of participants). The Ghana Co-operative Susu Collectors Association represents a coordinated network that has survived for generations.

The system's structural vulnerabilities are also its opportunity. Paper-based records are vulnerable to loss and fraud. Cash handling creates security risks for collectors. Bank of Ghana regulations currently prohibit susu lending — locking savings into non-interest-bearing accounts. Most importantly, the daily collection data that proves creditworthiness for millions of Ghanaians never reaches the formal credit system.

BRIDGE's Digital Susu Integration creates a bridge: digitize the collections, generate credit histories, connect those histories to formal lending products — while preserving the community relationships that make the system work. The susu collector is not a competitor to formal finance; she is the last-mile distribution network that formal finance cannot replicate.

05
Section FiveDiaspora Capital

$6.65 Billion Arriving Annually — 90% Going to Consumption

Ghana's 3 million diaspora is one of the country's largest capital sources. Almost none of it builds productive assets.

Ghana's estimated 3 million diaspora sent $6.65 billion in official remittances in 2024 — with an estimated $4.85 billion more flowing through informal channels, bringing total inflows to approximately $11.5 billion. This exceeds Ghana's FDI and is second only to oil revenues as a foreign exchange source.

The allocation problem is structural. High transfer fees (7% average on a $200 transfer, more than double the 3% SDG target) reduce funds at the point of receipt. Recipient households lack investment vehicles and financial literacy to deploy remittances productively. Currency volatility erodes GHS-denominated savings. The result: 90% of remittances fund immediate consumption — food, school fees, medical bills — rather than the assets, businesses, or investments that would build household security.

Diaspora Remittance Flows and Use Allocation
Total diaspora inflows reach $11.5B when informal channels are included. Of the official $6.65B, 90% funds consumption and only 10% reaches productive investment — the allocation gap BRIDGE's Diaspora Investment Gateway is designed to close.

Section SevenCross-Sector

Financial Inclusion as the Enabling Layer

Every sector in the BRIDGE portfolio is blocked by the same credit wall. Financial Inclusion is the key that unlocks all twelve.

Financial Inclusion is not one sector among twelve — it is the enabling layer beneath all of them. The agricultural processor cannot buy equipment without asset finance. The market vendor cannot scale inventory without working capital. The real estate buyer cannot access a mortgage without a credit history. The health entrepreneur cannot invest in clinic equipment without business finance.

BRIDGE's Trader Working Capital Facility, MSME Credit Guarantee, and Alternative Credit Scoring Platform are therefore not standalone financial inclusion ventures — they are the foundation infrastructure for every other sector investment. When BRIDGE deploys capital into Agriculture, Health, or Manufacturing, it is the Financial Inclusion sector that determines whether Ghanaian entrepreneurs can participate as owners rather than just workers.

Cross-Sector Impact

Agriculture: Smallholder input finance and cooperative working capital — MSME Credit Guarantee Facility is the delivery vehicle.
Infrastructure: Market vendor digital payments and vendor credit — Market Financial Services Platform provides the rails.
Housing: Mortgage-linked savings products and land purchase finance — Trader Working Capital graduates into asset accumulation.
Energy: Pay-as-you-go solar and clean cooking finance — microinsurance bundles and asset finance are the entry products.
