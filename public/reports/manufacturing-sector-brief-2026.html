Section OneContext

Half-Empty Factories and a Continental Market at the Door

Ghana's manufacturers are running at 30–40% capacity utilisation while AfCFTA opens a 1.4 billion consumer market 90 minutes away by air. The constraint is not demand — it is power cost, working capital, skills, and quality certification. BRIDGE addresses each systematically.

Ghana has manufacturing. Over 600,000 formal manufacturing workers, 1.5 million more in informal production, 500+ free zone enterprises, a 1D1F programme that has delivered 126 operational factories across the country. The industrial base exists. What is missing is the enabling layer that converts existing capacity into competitive exports.

The numbers tell a precise story. Average capacity utilisation across Ghanaian manufacturing is 30–40% — meaning the majority of installed equipment sits idle most of the time. Not because of absent demand: Ghana's own growing middle class and the AfCFTA continental market together represent more consumption than Ghanaian factories could serve even at full capacity in the near term. The utilisation gap is structural: power costs 2–3× what manufacturers pay in competing countries, working capital is expensive and scarce, technically trained workers are in short supply, and quality certification for export markets is largely inaccessible to SME manufacturers. Fix these four constraints and Ghana's existing manufacturing capacity can double its output without a single new factory.

"Ghana exports shea nuts at 1× value and imports shea butter cosmetics at 28×. It exports raw cocoa at 2× value and imports chocolate at 30×. The raw material is Ghanaian. The jobs, the processing, the brand value — they are not. BRIDGE's manufacturing portfolio is built around a single question: how do we capture more of that value chain in Ghana?"

Section TwoThe AfCFTA Moment

1.4 Billion Consumers, $3.4 Trillion GDP — Ghana Is Positioned to Serve Them

Ghana is the secretariat of AfCFTA. It has the institutional relationships, the port infrastructure, and the manufacturing base to be West Africa's production hub for the continental market. The window is now.

The African Continental Free Trade Area is not a future aspiration — it is an active trading framework with 54 signatory states, covering 1.4 billion people and a $3.4 trillion combined GDP. Tariff elimination schedules are already in effect for most product categories. Ghana, as the AfCFTA secretariat host, has a political and institutional advantage that no other country on the continent holds: it is at the centre of the policy conversations that will shape how the agreement is implemented.

The sectors where Ghana has the raw material advantage and needs processing investment to capture AfCFTA value are precisely the BRIDGE portfolio: agro-processing (cocoa, shea, cassava, plantain), pharmaceuticals, personal care and cosmetics, and building materials. Each of these starts with a Ghanaian resource, could be processed to a finished product in Ghana, and finds a continental market across AfCFTA borders. The value capture analysis is stark: a metric tonne of shea nuts exports at $600. The same tonne processed into refined cosmetic-grade shea butter exports at $3,500–5,000. Processed into premium personal care products, the value per tonne reaches $10,000–15,000. Ghana is currently exporting at the bottom of every one of these chains.

AfCFTA Export Opportunity: Current vs. Potential by Sector
Processed agri-foods currently represent 8% of manufacturing export value against a 38% AfCFTA-accessible potential. Pharmaceuticals at 4% against 18% potential. The gap across every sector is the same story: Ghana has the raw material, the institutional position, and the market access. It lacks the processing investment and quality certification infrastructure. BRIDGE addresses the latter two.

Section ThreeCost Competitiveness

Power and Policy: Ghana's Two Manufacturing Gaps

Ghana's manufacturers pay 2–3× the power cost of regional competitors. This single factor explains more of the capacity utilisation gap than any other. It is also the one BRIDGE can partially address through distributed commercial solar.

Ghana's industrial electricity costs $0.132/kWh. Ethiopia charges $0.052/kWh. Kenya charges $0.078/kWh. Senegal, $0.095/kWh. For energy-intensive manufacturing — food processing, textiles, pharmaceuticals, plastics — this differential translates directly into cost structures that cannot compete on export pricing even when every other factor is equal. A Ghanaian food processor and a Kenyan competitor with identical equipment, identical labour productivity, and identical raw material access will produce at different unit costs because of electricity alone.

BRIDGE's Energy portfolio's C&I Solar Installation Company directly addresses this for manufacturing clients — eliminating grid tariff exposure for the 30–60% of operating hours when solar can cover load. For manufacturers in the BRIDGE portfolio, cross-sector coordination between manufacturing investment and solar installation is built into the implementation design, not added as an afterthought.

Ghana's manufacturing share of GDP at 7.8% reflects both the underinvestment in processing and the competitiveness drag from energy costs. Section FourThe Shea Opportunity

A Case Study in Captured vs. Uncaptured Value

Ghana produces 40% of the world's shea. It captures less than 5% of the value. BRIDGE's Shea Value Addition venture is the clearest single illustration of what the manufacturing portfolio is for.

Ghana's shea sector is a parable for the entire manufacturing opportunity. Ghana produces approximately 400,000 metric tonnes of shea nuts annually — 40% of global supply. European and Asian cosmetics companies send buyers to Ghana, purchase raw nuts at $600–800 per tonne, ship them to refineries in the Netherlands, Germany, and Japan, process them into cosmetic-grade shea butter and personal care products, and sell them globally at $3,500–15,000 per tonne equivalent value. Ghana participates in the first 600 of those 15,000 dollars. The jobs, the processing infrastructure, the brand value, the export revenue — they leave with the raw material.

The Shea Value Addition venture captures this chain in Ghana. Processing infrastructure for crude and refined shea butter, cold-press extraction for cosmetic-grade product, certification for EU and US personal care markets, and a commercial partnership with diaspora-connected cosmetics brands that can access premium retail channels. Ghana already has the raw material advantage, the labour, and the land. BRIDGE adds the processing technology, quality standards, and market access that convert a commodity export into a finished product export.

Shea Value Chain: Value Multiplier at Each Processing Stage
Raw shea nuts export at 1× value. Crude shea butter reaches 2.8×. Refined cosmetic-grade butter: 4.5×. Personal care products: 12×. Premium retail: 28×. Ghana currently participates at the bottom of this chain. BRIDGE's Shea Value Addition venture targets 12–28× capture — investing in processing, certification, and the market access that makes premium positioning achievable.

Section SixCross-Sector

Manufacturing is Where Every Sector's Value Gets Captured

Cocoa without chocolate factories. Shea without cosmetics plants. Fish without canning lines. Ghana's raw material wealth is exported and returned as finished goods at 10–28× the price. Manufacturing is the value capture layer for every other sector BRIDGE invests in.

Cross-Sector Manufacturing Linkages

Agriculture: Every agro-processing venture is simultaneously an agricultural sector investment. Stable off-take from processing facilities is the demand certainty that justifies increased smallholder production investment. Value chain integration is the architecture.
Energy: C&I solar directly reduces the power cost disadvantage that constrains manufacturing competitiveness. Manufacturing Skills Academy and Solar Technician Training Academy share training infrastructure and diaspora technical expertise.
Housing: Building Materials Manufacturing serves Ghana's 1.8M-unit housing deficit. Lower input costs mean more affordable housing units. BRIDGE's construction oversight platform creates the quality standards that building materials manufacturers produce to.
Financial Inclusion: Manufacturing SMEs are among the primary targets for BRIDGE's SME lending and working capital products. Quality certification opens the formal finance access that informal manufacturers lack.
Creative Industries: Textile and Garment Manufacturing, Personal Care Products, and Shea Value Addition are simultaneously creative economy investments — building the Ghanaian manufacturing brands that cultural tourism and diaspora retail channels can sell.
