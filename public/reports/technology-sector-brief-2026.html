Section OneContext

Leapfrog or Left Behind

70% internet penetration. $68M in startup funding. 100+ tech hubs. And virtually no Series A capital in-country. Ghana's tech sector has infrastructure without depth.

Ghana's technology sector sits at an inflection point. On one side: the country ranks 3rd in West Africa for startup ecosystems, has built world-leading mobile money infrastructure, and in 2024 recorded $68 million in startup funding — a 152% increase over 2023. On the other: 60% of that capital flows to fintech alone, female founders receive less than 1% of total funding, and the Series A stage is virtually absent in-country, forcing growth-stage startups to relocate to Nairobi or Lagos to survive.

The connectivity picture is equally paradoxical. 70% internet penetration nationally collapses to 28% in rural areas. 4G coverage reaches 60% of the population, but only 15% actively use it. Fixed broadband penetration is below 0.7%. The infrastructure exists; the utilization does not.

"The gap is not access — it is utilization, commercialization, and scale. Ghana needs growth capital, diaspora expertise, and market platform infrastructure that converts potential into economic output."

BRIDGE enters with a structural advantage. The Kejetia Digital Platform — already in development as BRIDGE's flagship infrastructure venture — is simultaneously the most important technology venture in the portfolio and the proof-of-concept that positions BRIDGE as Ghana's leading market digitization operator. From that anchor, the BRIDGE Growth Fund, Tech Talent Bridge, and sector-specific equity portfolios build a diversified technology investment ecosystem.

Connectivity: Coverage vs. Utilisation vs. Rural Access
Ghana's 70% internet penetration masks sharp divergences: rural connectivity is 28%, 4G active usage is 15% despite 60% coverage, and fixed broadband reaches less than 1% of the population. The infrastructure is built; the activation is not.

Section TwoCapital

$68M In — Almost None Gets to Series A

Ghana startup funding grew 152% in 2024. The capital stays in pre-seed and seed. Growth-stage companies have one option: leave.

Ghana's startup capital market has two distinct phases separated by a cliff. Pre-seed and seed rounds are functional: hundreds of deals, growing angel networks, and active hub programs provide early-stage support. Then the market stops. Series A funding in-country is virtually nonexistent. The handful of companies that reach growth stage have three choices: bootstrap through constraints, accept unfavorable terms from foreign investors, or relocate headquarters to Nairobi or Lagos where the capital infrastructure exists.

The talent dimension compounds the capital problem. Ghana produces approximately 3,200 computer science graduates and 8,000 bootcamp graduates annually — far more than the estimated 11,000 formal tech employment positions. The resulting gap is not unemployment: it is emigration. An estimated 2,800 tech workers leave Ghana annually for remote work or physical relocation. The ecosystem trains the talent; the capital environment cannot retain it.

Ghana Startup Funding (2020–2024) and West Africa Share
Ghana funding grew from $12M (2020) to $68M (2024) — a 5.7x increase in four years. Ghana holds 14% of West Africa ecosystem funding, behind Nigeria (61%) but ahead of Senegal and Ivory Coast. Venture debt grew 431% year-over-year in 2024.
The Series A Cliff: Deal Count vs. Capital by Stage
Pre-seed deals number in the hundreds; Series A closes number in single digits. The cliff is structural — Ghana lacks the institutional capital infrastructure (pension funds, sovereign funds, development finance) that populates Series A rounds in more mature markets.

Section ThreeTalent

The Pipeline Exists — The Retention System Does Not

3,200 CS graduates and 8,000 bootcamp completions annually. An estimated 2,800 tech workers emigrate each year. Training without retention is a subsidy for foreign tech sectors.

Ghana's technical talent pipeline is genuine. The University of Ghana, KNUST, and Ashesi produce credible computer science graduates. ALX Africa has enrolled over 100,000 students in data science and software engineering. Over 100 tech hubs run training programs across the country. The bottleneck is not supply — it is the absence of the career pathways, compensation structures, and growth-stage companies that would retain this talent in-country.

The solution is not more training. It is more companies at the scale where senior technical talent can build careers with equity upside, global-calibre challenges, and compensation that competes with remote work opportunities. Every new growth-stage company BRIDGE helps capitalize is a retention infrastructure investment for the next cohort of technical graduates.

Tech Talent: Production vs. Retention vs. Emigration
Ghana produces 11,200 tech-capable graduates and bootcamp completions annually against approximately 11,000 formal tech employment positions. The surplus does not stay in Ghana — an estimated 2,800 tech workers emigrate annually for remote work or physical relocation.
Ecosystem Funding by Sector: The Fintech Concentration Risk
Fintech absorbs 60% of Ghana's startup ecosystem funding — creating concentration risk and leaving agtech, healthtech, edtech, and climate tech systematically undercapitalized despite demonstrated market demand in each. BRIDGE's sector-specific portfolios are designed to correct this distortion.
