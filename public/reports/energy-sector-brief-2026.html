Section OneContext

89% Electrified, 0.8% Renewable — and $924M Lost to Outages Each Year

Ghana is close to universal electrification. But the power it delivers is expensive, unreliable, and almost entirely fossil-fuelled. The transition to affordable, clean, reliable energy is not an aspiration — it is an economic survival requirement.

Ghana's electricity story has two chapters. The first is an achievement: 89% national electrification — one of the highest rates in Sub-Saharan Africa, built over decades of infrastructure investment. 5,507 MW of installed generation capacity serving 33 million people. A national grid that reaches most corners of the country. By infrastructure reach, Ghana has succeeded.

The second chapter is a structural failure. 66% of that capacity runs on thermal generation — gas and oil — at costs that have risen 352% cumulatively over sixteen years. The legacy debt from power purchase agreements has accumulated to $2.1 billion in arrears. Industrial electricity costs $0.132/kWh — 2–3× what manufacturers in Ethiopia, Kenya, or Senegal pay. And when the grid fails — which it does regularly — the economic cost runs to $320–924 million per year, 2–6% of GDP, concentrated in manufacturing, SMEs, and agriculture. The power sector is simultaneously Ghana's greatest infrastructure achievement and one of its heaviest economic burdens.

"Ghana built a grid that reaches everyone. Now it needs to fill that grid with power that is affordable, reliable, and clean. Solar has crossed the cost crossover point — it is now cheaper for commercial and industrial users than the grid tariff. The economics have shifted. The transition is overdue."
Current Generation Mix vs. 2030 Renewable Target
66% thermal dominance against a 0.8% renewable share (non-hydro) reveals how far Ghana is from its own 10% RE target by 2030. The installed solar pipeline (188 MW operational, 200 MW under construction) shows movement — but from near zero. BRIDGE's portfolio accelerates the distributed transition that utility-scale procurement alone cannot achieve.

Section TwoEconomic Cost

$924M Lost Every Year to Unreliable Power

Power outages are not inconveniences. They are a tax on every Ghanaian manufacturer, farmer, and small business. $320–924 million per year — 2–6% of GDP — transferred from productive enterprise to backup generation, spoiled goods, and lost output.

The economic cost of power unreliability falls heaviest on the sectors BRIDGE focuses on: manufacturing, where continuous process industries (food processing, pharmaceuticals, plastics) cannot operate intermittently without quality loss; SMEs, which cannot afford the industrial generators that large enterprises deploy; agriculture and cold chain, where power failure means spoilage and the post-harvest losses that Ghana's agricultural sector already suffers acutely; and health facilities, where outages are not economic events but clinical ones.

Commercial and industrial solar eliminates this cost directly. A C&I solar installation with battery backup converts an unreliable grid connection into a reliable baseload — and at $0.048/kWh LCOE versus $0.132/kWh grid tariff, it pays for itself in under four years. The economics of the transition have already arrived. BRIDGE's C&I Solar Installation Company operationalises them at scale for the commercial and manufacturing clients who most need the certainty.

Economic Cost of Power Outages by Sector
Manufacturing bears the highest absolute outage cost ($145–380M/year), but the impact is felt across every sector of the economy. SMEs — which cannot afford generator backup — absorb $95–260M in annual losses. The $320–924M total represents a permanent drag on Ghana's industrial competitiveness that commercial solar directly eliminates.

Section ThreeThe Clean Cooking Gap

Six Thousand Deaths a Year from a Solvable Problem

60% of Ghanaian households cook on charcoal or firewood. 6,500 people die annually from indoor air pollution. Clean cooking is not an environmental issue — it is a public health emergency with a commercially viable solution.

Charcoal and firewood dominate household energy for the majority of Ghanaians regardless of whether they are grid-connected. The electricity grid reaches 89% of households for lighting and appliances — but clean cooking requires either LPG, biogas, or improved cookstove adoption, and each of these faces its own distribution, affordability, and behaviour change challenges. The result is a persistent gap: 60% of households, disproportionately in rural and peri-urban areas, exposed to indoor air pollution levels that the WHO classifies as a major health hazard.

The 6,500 annual deaths from household air pollution represent a human cost more acute than many better-funded health interventions address. The economic cost — lost productivity, healthcare burden, women and children's time spent on fuel collection — compounds it. BRIDGE's Clean Cooking Distribution Network is not an NGO programme: it is a commercially structured distribution business, partnering with Ghana Cylinder Manufacturing Company and LPG distributors, built on the insight that reaching the last mile profitably requires subsidised first-cylinder programmes and credit-linked refill subscription models that make clean cooking financially accessible, not just physically available.

Clean vs. Polluting Fuel Access by Location
Urban areas have 65% clean fuel access — but peri-urban (42%) and rural (15%) households remain predominantly on charcoal and firewood. The 6,500 annual deaths from indoor air pollution fall almost entirely in these communities. BRIDGE's distribution model targets peri-urban density first — maximum reach at minimum cost — before extending into rural distribution.
Solar Tariff vs. Grid Cost: The Crossover Moment
Solar LCOE has fallen below the grid tariff — the crossover that makes commercial solar economically compelling without subsidy. C&I solar at $0.048/kWh versus the grid at $0.132/kWh delivers a 64% cost advantage with 25-year guaranteed pricing against a grid tariff that has risen 352% in 16 years. The economics have shifted permanently.

Section FourThe Solar Advantage

All of Ghana is a Solar Market — the North is the Best One

4.5–6 kWh/m²/day across every region of Ghana. No location in the country is too cloudy or too shaded for viable solar generation. The resource constraint is not irradiance — it is installation capacity, equipment quality, and financing access.

Ghana's solar resource is exceptional and uniform. Even Greater Accra — the least irradiant region — receives 4.7 kWh/m²/day, above the international threshold for economically viable solar development. The Upper East and Upper West receive 5.8 kWh/m²/day — among the best in West Africa. Every region of Ghana, from the coast to the Sahel fringe, is a viable solar market. The constraint is not the sun. It is the trained technicians to install systems, the quality-assured equipment to fill them, and the financing structures that make them accessible to the commercial clients and rural communities who need them most.

BRIDGE's Solar Technician Training Academy and Quality Equipment Distribution venture address both constraints simultaneously — building the human capital and supply chain infrastructure that determines whether Ghana's solar transition reaches its potential or stalls at a niche market for large corporates with procurement departments.

Ghana Solar Irradiance Potential by Region
Every region of Ghana exceeds the minimum viable threshold for solar generation. The Northern savannah regions receive 5.6–5.8 kWh/m²/day — a genuine competitive advantage for off-grid mini-grid development and the 2 million Volta Lake community residents who remain without grid access. Solar is not a southern urban product — it is a national energy solution.

Section SixCross-Sector

Energy is Infrastructure for Everything Else

There is no BRIDGE agricultural intervention that does not require cold chain power. No manufacturing investment that does not depend on reliable electricity. No health facility that functions well through outages. Energy is the foundational layer on which every sector's performance rests.

Cross-Sector Energy Linkages

Agriculture: Solar Cold Storage Network directly addresses the post-harvest loss crisis. Irrigation pumping, agro-processing, and rural market electrification all depend on reliable, affordable power.
Manufacturing: The $320–924M annual outage cost falls hardest on manufacturers. C&I solar eliminates it. Energy cost competitiveness is the single largest factor closing Ghana's manufacturing gap with regional peers.
Health Systems: Health facilities on unreliable grid power cannot maintain cold chain for vaccines, cannot run diagnostic equipment consistently, cannot retain qualified staff. Solar backup is not a luxury — it is a clinical standard.
Education: School electrification enables evening classes, computer labs, and digital learning. Solar Technician Training Academy is itself an education sector investment — producing 500+ certified graduates per year.
Financial Inclusion: Pay-as-you-go solar and clean cooking financing are financial inclusion products. Mobile money-linked energy payments drive financial account activity and credit history for previously unbanked households.
