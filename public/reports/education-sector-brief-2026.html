Section OneContext

Credentials Without Outcomes

86% primary enrollment. 22.3% university graduate unemployment. Ghana's education system invests enormously — and the return is a generation locked out of the economy it built.

Ghana's education statistics tell two stories simultaneously. Enrollment rates show a system expanding rapidly: 86% in primary, 57% at secondary, nearly universal Free SHS access. By the metrics of access, Ghana is succeeding. By the metrics of outcome — employment, income, economic mobility — the system is failing the very students it is educating.

Youth unemployment stands at 12.6%. Among tertiary graduates — those who completed the longest, most expensive educational journey — 22.3% experience unemployment spells. Meanwhile, 80–90% of the skills the Ghanaian economy actually uses are delivered through informal apprenticeship: master craftspeople training the next generation outside any formal recognition or certification system, with no quality assurance, no credential, and no pathway to formal employment.

"The formal education system produces graduates misaligned with labor market needs while the informal apprenticeship system — delivering 80–90% of economically-used skills — operates without recognition, certification, or quality assurance."

BRIDGE's education interventions are not about building more classrooms. They are about reconnecting the education system to economic outcomes — through TVET partnerships that link training to employment, scholarship programs tied to service commitments in Ghana, and apprenticeship recognition that brings 1.5 million informal learners into a credentialed system that values what they already know.

The Education Funnel: Enrollment vs. Economic Outcome
Enrollment drops from 86% at primary to 21% at tertiary — and youth employment reaches only 51% of the relevant cohort. The education investment does not translate to economic participation. TVET enrollment at just 5% of the cohort is the sharpest illustration of the skills pathway problem.

Section TwoThe Paradox

More Education, More Unemployment

University graduates in Ghana are more likely to be unemployed than those who left school at JHS. This is not a coincidence — it is a structural signal.

Ghana's education–employment paradox is measurable and severe. Youth with no formal education have an unemployment rate of approximately 6.2%. JHS graduates face 9.4%. SHS graduates 11.2%. University graduates? 22.3% experience unemployment spells — the highest of any education level. More time in school correlates with worse employment outcomes.

The reason is structural. University curricula are designed around academic knowledge production, not labor market readiness. Employers consistently report that graduates lack practical skills, workplace behavior, and the sector-specific capabilities that make someone immediately productive. The TVET system that would deliver those skills enrolls only 50,049 students nationally — less than 5% of what industry needs — and carries deep social stigma as a "second-choice" pathway.

Unemployment Rate by Education Level
University graduates (22.3% unemployment) fare worse than every other education level — including those who never completed primary school. TVET graduates at 8.1% outperform SHS completers. The evidence case for TVET investment is written in these numbers.

Section ThreeTVET

The System That Works — Ignored

82% of Ghana's workforce skills come from informal apprenticeship. The formal TVET system enrolls 50,000 students. Industry needs 280,000 trained workers annually.

Ghana's Technical and Vocational Education and Training system faces a contradiction: TVET graduates have better employment outcomes than university graduates, yet TVET enrollment is a fraction of what it needs to be, and the system carries persistent social stigma. Parents and students view TVET as a consolation prize for those who failed academic pathways. The government has historically underfunded it relative to university education.

The informal apprenticeship system fills the gap — but invisibly. An estimated 1.5 million Ghanaians are learning trades under master craftspeople with no formal recognition, no certification that employers outside the informal sector can verify, and no pathway into the formal economy. These learners possess real, valuable, economically critical skills. The system simply cannot see them.

TVET Scale Gap and Skills Source Breakdown
Formal TVET enrolls 50K students against industry demand for 280K+ trained workers annually. The informal apprenticeship system trains an estimated 1.5 million — but without recognition, certification, or formal economy access. The Ghana Skills Development Fund ($200M, World Bank) is the co-financing vehicle BRIDGE builds on.
Infrastructure Deficit: School Resources vs. Targets
Only 25% of primary schools have electricity. 15% have ICT labs. 31% of early childhood teachers are certified. Household computer ownership stands at 28% nationally and 14% in rural areas. Digital skills programs must account for the device and connectivity gap they are building on top of.

Section FourSkills Demand

What Industry Needs — And Cannot Find

Construction, digital, health, manufacturing, agri-processing — every sector in BRIDGE's portfolio has a workforce gap. Education is not a standalone intervention; it is the upstream constraint on every other sector's growth.

Every BRIDGE sector brief surfaces the same underlying constraint: trained workers. The agriculture sector needs agronomists, cold chain technicians, and cooperative managers. The construction boom needs certified tradespeople. The health system needs nurses and community health workers. The technology sector needs developers and data scientists. In every case, the skills pipeline is the binding constraint on how fast the sector can grow.

Ghana's Skills Development Fund — backed by $200M in World Bank support — exists precisely to address this. BRIDGE's TVET Partnership Program and Skills Bootcamps are designed to operate within this institutional framework, using the Fund's co-financing to scale interventions that would be unaffordable from BRIDGE capital alone.

Skills Demand vs. Graduate Supply by Sector
Every sector shows demand outpacing supply — with manufacturing (78 vs 29), construction (85 vs 35), and ICT (72 vs 28) showing the sharpest gaps. These are not abstract statistics: they are the direct upstream constraint on BRIDGE's infrastructure, technology, and manufacturing sector investments.
